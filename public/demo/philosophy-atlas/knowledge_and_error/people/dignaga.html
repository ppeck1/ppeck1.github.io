Answers: What is knowledge?

Knowledge as Valid Cognition (Pramana)

Knowledge is the product of valid means of cognition, which Dignaga reduces to two: perception (of bare particulars) and inference (of general features). Each means has its proper object; error arises from confusing or misapplying them.

Pramana (Means of Valid Cognition)Justification

Dignaga's apoha theory of meaning is technical; summarizing pramana without it loses how he handles concepts and error.

Historical setting

Dignaga’s work emerges from a highly technical Indian debate culture. The question is not generic “knowledge” but pramana: reliable means of cognition. He narrows valid cognition to perception and inference, reshaping Buddhist logic and later Tibetan scholastic traditions.

Perception and inference

Dignaga distinguishes direct perception from conceptual construction and develops a theory of inference that depends on reliable marks. His apoha, or exclusion, theory of meaning explains universals not as real shared essences but through exclusion of what does not belong.

Cautions

Pramana theory includes epistemic, logical, linguistic, and soteriological dimensions. Comparisons with analytic epistemology are useful, but Dignaga’s framework is not just a premodern version of justified true belief.

Historical setting

Dignāga is central to Buddhist epistemology in the Indian scholastic world of the fifth and sixth centuries CE. His work reorganizes debate about valid cognition, perception, inference, and language.

Why this thinker reasoned this way

The analysis separates kinds of warrant: perception and inference do different work. Conceptual construction and exclusion become central to explaining linguistic cognition.

Texts and transmission

The Pramāṇasamuccaya survives incompletely in Sanskrit and through Tibetan transmission and commentaries. Reconstruction is textually demanding.

Justified True Belief Is Not Knowledge

Dignaga grounds knowledge in the reliable operation of cognitive instruments (perception, inference); Gettier shows that on a justification-based analysis, reliable-seeming justification can still misfire — two different diagnoses of when cognition counts as knowing.
