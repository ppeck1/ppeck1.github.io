Epistemic Caution and the Two Truths

Nagarjuna does not offer a modern theory of justified belief. His epistemic importance is diagnostic: attempts to ground knowledge, causation, motion, identity, or proof in intrinsic nature generate contradictions. The two truths allow conventional inquiry, language, and correction to function while refusing to treat any criterion as self-grounding. This makes Madhyamaka a disciplined anti-reification tool rather than a global denial that claims can be warranted in ordinary and soteriological contexts. Comparisons with skepticism should be marked carefully; Madhyamaka is not Pyrrhonism under another name.

Historical setting

Nāgārjuna is associated with the formation of Madhyamaka in the early centuries CE within Indian Buddhist scholastic and monastic debate. The historical biography is sparse, while later traditions amplify it through devotional and lineage memory.

Why this thinker reasoned this way

His arguments address a recurring pressure inside Buddhist analysis: how to deny permanent essence without turning dependent life into nihilism. Dependent origination, conventional designation, and emptiness must be read together.

Texts and transmission

The Mūlamadhyamakakārikā survives through Sanskrit fragments and manuscripts, Tibetan and Chinese translations, and extensive commentarial traditions. Translation choices around śūnyatā, svabhāva, and the two truths materially affect interpretation.
