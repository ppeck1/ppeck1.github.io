Siddhartha Gautama (the Buddha)

Founder of Buddhism. His analysis of the person into impermanent aggregates and his doctrine of no-self (anatta) reframed the self as a process to be seen through rather than a substance to be defended.

Core problems and positions

Answers: What is the self?

No Enduring Self (Anatta)

There is no permanent, independent self. What we take to be a self is a bundle of five impermanent aggregates (form, sensation, perception, mental formations, consciousness). Clinging to a fixed self causes suffering.

No-Self (Anatta)Bundle Theory

Anatta denies a permanent self, not the conventional functioning person; it should not be read as nihilism about persons.

Historical setting

The Buddha’s analysis of self belongs to a project of ending suffering. The five aggregates are examined because clinging to them as “I” or “mine” generates instability, craving, and distress. This is not a denial that persons conventionally function; it is a denial that an enduring, independent self can be found in body, feeling, perception, formations, or consciousness.

No-self and dependent arising

No-self works together with dependent origination. Experience occurs through conditions; identity is assembled through habit, craving, memory, naming, and appropriation. The teaching is diagnostic: when the aggregates are seen as impermanent and not-self, the grip of misidentification loosens.

Cautions and misreadings

A common error is to read anatta as “you do not exist.” A better atlas phrasing is that no independent, permanent, self-owning essence is found under analysis. Conventional persons, ethical responsibility, memory, practice, and liberation remain intelligible within conditioned continuity.

Historical setting

The historical Buddha is placed in the north Indian śramaṇa environment of the first millennium BCE. Traditional biographies and critical historical reconstruction overlap but are not identical.

Why this thinker reasoned this way

The teaching responds to suffering through a disciplined analysis of causes, attachment, impermanence, and practice. No-self is embedded in a therapeutic and ethical path rather than offered as an isolated metaphysical slogan.

Texts and transmission

Teachings were transmitted orally and preserved through multiple canons and recensions. Early Buddhist texts, later schools, and devotional biographies should be distinguished.
