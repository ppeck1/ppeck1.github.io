Core problems and positions

Answers: Is change possible without fixed essences?

Emptiness Makes Change Possible

Precisely because things lack inherent, fixed essence, they can arise, change, and cease through dependent origination. A thing with a permanent essence could never change; emptiness is the condition of becoming, not its denial.

Emptiness (Sunyata)Dependent Origination

Emptiness is not nihilism; it denies inherent essence, not conventional change.

Historical setting

Nagarjuna writes from within Buddhist debates about dependent origination, not from a detached metaphysical curiosity about change. Abhidharma analysis had decomposed persons and objects into events and factors, but that analysis could itself harden into a new realism about ultimate constituents. Madhyamaka pressure-tests that hardening. The change problem is therefore therapeutic and logical at once: if things possessed self-existence, they could not depend, arise, cease, or function. Emptiness names the absence of that frozen essence, not a hidden substance behind events.

Doctrine architecture

The central architecture is a two-way lock: what is dependently originated is empty of inherent existence, and what is empty can function through dependence. This makes change intelligible without positing either permanent substances or sheer chaos. Nagarjuna repeatedly uses reductio arguments to show that intrinsic production, intrinsic cessation, intrinsic motion, and intrinsic identity collapse under analysis. The point is not that nothing exists; it is that things exist conventionally through relations, designation, causes, conditions, and use.

Cautions and misreadings

A major risk is reading Madhyamaka as nihilism. That flattens the two-truths structure and turns a critique of essence into a denial of conventional reality. A second risk is treating emptiness as a new absolute. Nagarjuna explicitly warns against reifying emptiness itself. In this atlas, Nagarjuna should be linked to process and becoming through dependent arising, but not treated as a Greek flux theorist in Buddhist vocabulary.

Historical setting

Nāgārjuna is associated with the formation of Madhyamaka in the early centuries CE within Indian Buddhist scholastic and monastic debate. The historical biography is sparse, while later traditions amplify it through devotional and lineage memory.

Why this thinker reasoned this way

His arguments address a recurring pressure inside Buddhist analysis: how to deny permanent essence without turning dependent life into nihilism. Dependent origination, conventional designation, and emptiness must be read together.

Texts and transmission

The Mūlamadhyamakakārikā survives through Sanskrit fragments and manuscripts, Tibetan and Chinese translations, and extensive commentarial traditions. Translation choices around śūnyatā, svabhāva, and the two truths materially affect interpretation.
